How to free up Gmail storage safely

Guide · last updated 20 November 2026

Your Gmail account shares its storage with Google Drive and Google Photos. When it fills up, the largest items are usually attachments. This guide shows how to find them, remove them safely, and confirm the result.

Before you delete anything

Decide what you need to keep. Messages from people you correspond with, receipts, and anything with legal or tax value are worth checking first. Items you star, label, or reply to are often the ones you want to keep, and many clean-up tools protect them by default.

Deleting a message in Gmail moves it to Trash. Trash keeps messages for a limited time, which Google has described as 30 days, so a mistake is usually recoverable for a while. Permanent removal happens later, when Trash is emptied or the 30-day period ends.

Step 1: Find the big messages

Most storage is used by a small number of large messages. Gmail search can narrow the list. Type a size operator into the search box, such as larger:10M for messages over about 10 megabytes, or has:attachment larger:5M for large messages that carry attachments. Gmail's search results are not sorted by size, so these operators are the practical way to focus on the biggest items. The complete set of operators is in the guide to finding large emails.

Look at the results with a critical eye. Newsletters with large images, old photos sent to a group, and repeated copies of the same file in long reply chains are common sources of storage use.

Step 2: Remove mailing lists you no longer read

A large share of storage often comes from a few mailing lists or automated senders. For messages that offer it, Gmail shows an unsubscribe option near the sender's name. Using it stops future mail, which helps prevent the problem from returning. Be cautious with unknown links in messages, and prefer the unsubscribe option Gmail itself shows.

To judge which senders to remove, search by sender, for example from:newsletter, and look at how many messages you have and how many you open.

Step 3: Delete in batches and check the result

  1. Run a narrow search, such as has:attachment larger:5M older_than:2y.
  2. Read the list, and open any message you are unsure about.
  3. Select the messages you no longer need, and choose the Trash icon.
  4. Repeat for the next group.

Working in batches keeps mistakes small. It also makes it easier to notice when a search returns something you did not expect.

Step 4: Empty Trash and Spam

Items in Trash still count toward your storage until they are removed. Open the Trash folder and choose the option to empty it. Spam works the same way. Gmail removes items in Spam and Trash automatically after a period of time, but emptying them yourself frees space sooner. Check that Trash contains nothing you still want before you empty it, because that step cannot be undone.

Tip: Trash and Spam can hold large messages you forgot about. Search with in:anywhere to include them when you look for big items.

Step 5: Check the result, and remember Drive

Google's storage totals can take a while to update after you delete things. Give it some time, then check the storage page in your Google account. The page shows usage across Gmail, Drive, and Photos, which is useful because the three services share one quota. Large files in Google Drive are often a bigger factor than mail, so review those too. Our guide to duplicate and large files in Google Drive covers that side.

Stopping the problem from coming back

Filters can help. Gmail lets you create filters that label, archive, or delete messages that match a search, such as a sender or a size. Test a filter on a few messages first, because filters apply to new mail and can hide messages you need. Setting up retention habits, such as deleting large attachments once you have saved them elsewhere, is often more effective than one large clean-up.

Making it easier with Gmail Storage Lens

Gmail Storage Lens is an open-source project that shows what is using your Gmail and Drive storage, and helps you plan a clean-up with a preview before anything changes. It runs in your browser, reads message metadata only, and sends data only to Google. Some features are still being built, so check the project for the current status before you rely on them.
